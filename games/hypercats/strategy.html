Hypercats 4D

Strategy Guide

Four dimensions sound frightening. They aren't, once you know which three facts do all the work.

1. The rules, in plain words

The board is a hypercube: several ordinary 3D cubes lined up along a fourth direction. On a 4⁴ board there are 4 cubes, each 4 cells wide, deep and tall, for 256 cells in all. You place one cat per colored region, so a 4⁴ board holds exactly 4 cats.

  • One per region. Every colored region gets exactly one cat.
  • One per slab. No two cats share a cube, a floor, a row or a column. On a 4⁴ board that means every cube, every floor, every row and every column holds exactly one cat.
  • No touching. Cats can't be neighbors, not even diagonally, and that includes the cubes on either side of yours.

Rows, columns and floors aren't lines here. Each is a whole slab that runs through every cube. "Floor 2" means floor 2 of cube 1, cube 2, cube 3 and cube 4 together, and only one of those 64 cells gets a cat.

2. Finding your way around

Every cell has four numbers: its cube (the fourth dimension), its floor (height, counted from the bottom), its row and its column. When you pick a floor in the app and look straight down, you're looking at a flat grid of rows and columns, just like a 2D puzzle.

A good habit is to think one cube at a time and one floor at a time. Every cube has exactly one cat, so each cube is its own little puzzle that just has to agree with its neighbors.

3. Three facts that do all the work

Fact one: count the slabs

There are as many cats as cubes, floors, rows and columns. So nothing can be left empty: every cube needs its cat, every floor needs its cat, and so on. Most solving is just asking "where can this one go?" until only one place is left.

Fact two: touching only means the full diagonal

This is the big one. Two cats can never share a cube, floor, row or column. So any two cats already differ in all four numbers. That means they can only touch if every number differs by exactly one: the next cube and the next floor and the next row and the next column, all at once.

A placed cat rules out its own cube, floor, row, column and region, plus just 16 extra cells: the full diagonals (cube ±1, floor ±1, row ±1, column ±1). Everything else that "touches" it was already ruled out by the slabs.

Fact three: neighboring cubes have to stretch apart

Look at the cats in two neighboring cubes, say cube 2 and cube 3. If their floors, rows and columns were each only one apart, they'd touch. So in at least one of those three directions, the two cats must be two or more apart. This quietly removes a lot of options, especially near the middle of the board.

4. Techniques, easiest first

These are listed in the order the game uses to rate puzzles. Easy puzzles fall to the first two. Experts need all of them, and sometimes a long chain of what-ifs.

Free cats

A region of a single cell is a free cat. Take it and let the eliminations ripple outward. Small regions of two or three cells are nearly as good: work them out early.

Last cell standing

If a region, cube, floor, row or column has only one cell left that isn't ruled out, the cat goes there. With assist on, ruled-out cells fade, so this shows up as one cell glowing alone in its slab.

Confinement

If every remaining cell of a region sits inside one slab, that slab's cat belongs to the region. Cross out every other cell of that slab, in every cube. It works in reverse too: if every remaining cell of a cube (or floor, row or column) belongs to one region, cross out the rest of that region.

The most useful version is a region that lives inside one cube. Then that cube's cat is the region's cat, and the whole rest of the cube is empty.

Pairs and trios

Confinement scales up. If two regions are both squeezed into the same two cubes, those two cubes' cats belong to them, so every other region's cells in those two cubes are out. The same goes for three regions in three floors, and so on.

What if?

Pick a cell and imagine a cat there. Would it leave some region, cube, floor, row or column with nowhere to go? Then it can't be a cat: mark it ✕. Good cells to test are ones that sit next to a small region, because a single cat's diagonal shadow can wipe out every cell of a two- or three-cell region at once.

Remember fact two when you test: a cat's shadow in the neighboring cubes is only its 16 full diagonals. You don't need to picture the fourth dimension, just "one step in every direction".

Longer chains

On expert puzzles and 5⁴ bosses you may need two what-ifs in a row: "if a cat goes here, then that region is forced there, and then this floor is empty". Take it slowly and use ✕ marks as notes. There's always a logical path. You never have to guess.

5. Using the app well

  • Tap once for ✕, twice for a cat. Hold a cell to clear its ✕. Mark freely: a ✕ costs nothing, but a wrong cat costs one of your three hearts.
  • Leave assist on while you learn. It fades cells your cats have already ruled out and turns cells red when they touch a cat in a neighboring cube. That red glow is fact two, drawn for you.
  • Solve floor by floor. Pick a floor to look straight down at it. The floors above disappear, so you can reach the middle of the cube, and each floor reads like a flat puzzle.
  • Flip between cubes often. The Cube buttons move through the fourth dimension, and a cat icon marks each cube that already has its cat. Most confinements jump out when you compare neighboring cubes.
  • Drag to turn, pinch to zoom. The arrow button puts the view back if you get lost.
  • Save hints for the bosses. You get two per puzzle. Each one reveals a cat, in your current cube if it can.

6. Levels, bosses and the daily

Levels never run out, and everyone gets the same puzzle for the same level number. The first three are small 3⁴ warm-ups. After that, levels come in blocks of ten: easier breathers mixed in with harder puzzles, and a boss at the end of each block. From level 23 through level 300, bosses are on a bigger 5⁴ board with five cats.

The daily is one shared puzzle per day, and it gets harder through the week: easy on Monday and Tuesday, medium on Wednesday and Thursday, hard on Friday and Saturday, and expert on Sunday. Solve it every day to keep your streak.

7. When you're stuck: a checklist

  1. Is any region down to one cell? Any cube, floor, row or column?
  2. Does a region fit inside a single cube, floor, row or column? Cross out the rest of that slab.
  3. Is a cube, floor, row or column covered by a single region? Cross out the rest of that region.
  4. Do two regions share the same two cubes (or floors, rows, columns)? Clear everything else from them.
  5. Find the smallest unsolved region. For each cell nearby, ask: would a cat here wipe it out?
  6. Still stuck? Try a what-if on a cell in the most crowded cube, and follow it two steps.
